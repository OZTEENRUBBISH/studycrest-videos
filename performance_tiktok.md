### What it says
- The one simulation with a reading had about 160 times the views of the better ad (19,486 against 121). Simulations are doing the job they were given: reach.
- People watched the simulation for about 9 seconds on average and the ads for about 1 second. The ads are being swiped past almost at once; their opening frame is the cover, then a plain card.
- Likes on the simulation are about 0.5% of views, which is low. Most viewers leave before the turn in the middle, so the first 3 to 9 seconds matter most.
- With one simulation measured, there is no evidence yet on what-if against second-by-second, or on topic. Next week's review will have about 20 simulations to compare.

### Changes to the plan
- Order of the plan kept (the owner approved the first 20). No kind is being cut on one data point.
- New ideas added this week lean on what the gravity video had: a planet-scale "what if" with a one-line question and a visible, physical consequence in an everyday or striking setting (the Moon as close as the space station; falling into Jupiter), plus one real event with a physical twist (Tacoma Narrows Bridge).
- For every new simulation: the surprising claim is now stated or shown inside the first 6 to 9 seconds, not held for the middle.
- Worth the owner's attention: the ad format. Three sends are too few to judge, but a 1-second average watch suggests trying one of the other ad styles in generator/README.md (low-poly story, whiteboard explainer) in the 12:00pm slot.

### What it says
- Of the simulations with a settled reading (two days or more), the eight what-ifs had 553 to 884 views, middle value 664; the four real events had 413 to 589, middle value 535. What-ifs hold the top seven places. The gap is real but modest (about a quarter more views).
- What the top what-ifs share: the question puts the viewer's own body or the ground under their feet into one strange physical situation (seeing radiation, stepping into space, falling through the Earth, the planet stopping), and people watch for 9 to 10 seconds. Real events are watched for 5 to 8 seconds; the slowest-starting story (Titanic, 5.4 s) is last.
- Likes per view are best on Apollo 13 (3.2%) and the Moon (2.9%), then space and radiation (about 2.5%). So real events are not worthless: fewer people see them, but those who do like them at least as often.
- Ads: 120 to 337 views, middle value 148, watched for 1 to 3 seconds. The maths ads (product rule, expanding) are watched longest; law and engineering got no likes at all.
- One simulation broke out in nine days (gravity) and nothing else has come within a hundredth of it, so volume and variety of simple, planet-scale or body-scale questions remains the best bet.

### Changes to the plan
- Order of the existing plan kept. Two more made tonight from the top (lift cable, Pompeii).
- New ideas added from now on: about three what-ifs for every real event (was two to one), and each what-if must put "you" in the picture in the first line. Real events are kept to ones with a single dramatic physical turn that can be shown in the first 6 seconds, not slow build-ups.
- For every simulation, real events included: open on the most striking moment and the surprising claim, then go back to the start.
- Ads: new spot-the-mistake reels are maths or physics for now (the best-watched subjects); law and engineering are rested.
